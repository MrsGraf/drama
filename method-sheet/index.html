Method Sheet · Drama Analysis

Analysing Dramatic Interaction

Understand what happens, trace how the interaction develops and explain how dramatic techniques create meaning.

Interaction & CommunicationStage Directions & PerformanceUse of Language
01 · Understand02 · Development03 · Techniques04 · Writing

Core question: What does the interaction reveal about the characters, their relationships and what is at stake — and how does the playwright make this visible?

Link your observations: What happens? → How is it presented? → What does it mean for the relationship, scene or audience?

01

Start with meaning

Understanding Dramatic Interaction

Who wants what? What is at stake?

Characters & relationships

  • What do the characters want, believe, fear or expect?
  • What is their relationship? Who has power or influence?
  • What do their reactions tell us beyond their statements?

Tension & subtext

  • Are their expectations or values compatible?
  • Do words and behaviour contradict each other?
  • What is implied but not openly expressed?
Does every dramatic interaction involve an open conflict?

No. Dramatic tension can arise from conflicting expectations, unequal power or unspoken feelings (subtext). An interaction may also develop through affection, persuasion, withdrawal, growing understanding or a change in perspective. Look beyond an obvious argument.

Your first analytical step: Form a provisional interpretation of the interaction. Refine it through close reading rather than treating your first impression as fact.

02

Trace change

Dramatic Development & Structure

How does the interaction unfold?

Phase 1

Opening

What starts the exchange? What intentions, relationships or expectations are present?

Phase 2

Development

How do statements, responses and actions change the exchange or mood?

Phase 3

Critical Moment

Is there a significant statement, reaction, insight, climax or turning point?

Phase 4

Outcome

What changes, remains unresolved or becomes more apparent?

Flexible framework, not a fixed formula.

Not every scene has four distinct phases, a clear escalation or one turning point. Some scenes contain several changes; others conclude quietly. Identify meaningful developments rather than forcing a scene into a predetermined pattern.

Consider shifts in tone, attitudes, relationships, control, tension or understanding. Explain how interaction, stage directions and language make these shifts apparent.

03

Investigate the presentation

Analysing Dramatic Techniques

How does the playwright create meaning?

Choose what matters — but follow the task.

Consider interaction, stage directions and use of language. If all three are specified in the examination task, address all three using relevant evidence. You do not need to give them equal attention or write three separate sections. Explain how the techniques work together instead of ticking off a checklist.

A · Interaction & Communication
  • Turn-taking: who starts, answers, dominates or speaks at length?
  • Responses: does a character acknowledge, ignore, resist, evade or develop the other's point?
  • Questions, commands and interruptions: who steers or challenges the conversation?
  • Silence, hesitation and avoidance: what remains unspoken?
  • Patterns and changes: does communication become closer, more distant, confrontational or cooperative?

Explain what the interaction reveals about relationships, intentions and dramatic development.

B · Stage Directions & Performance
  • Gestures, facial expressions, posture and movement: what do bodies reveal beyond speech?
  • Positioning, distance, entrances and exits: what relationships or power dynamics become visible?
  • Pauses, delivery and non-verbal reactions: do they support or contradict spoken words?
  • Props, costumes, setting and atmosphere: do material or visual details contribute to meaning?

Use the playwright's written directions as evidence; imagined acting choices are possibilities, not textual facts.

C · Use of Language

The familiar toolkit from the three guided analyses. Examine how language functions between the characters — not merely whether a device is present.

Syntax & sentence structure
Short or clipped sentences; complex or lengthy explanations; unfinished sentences, rhetorical questions, exclamations and parallel structures. Ask whether the syntax conveys urgency, distance, hesitation, confidence or pressure.
Tone
Serious, detached, hopeful, playful, affectionate, ironic, mocking, provocative, worried or aggressive. Show what produces the tone and whether it shifts during the exchange.
Word choice & register
Formal or colloquial language, emotionally charged words, semantic fields, positive or negative connotations, pronouns, terms of address and contrasts between characters. Explain how choices reveal identity, attitudes or power.
Selected stylistic devices
Meaningful repetition, contrasts, imagery, metaphors, similes, hyperbole, understatement and irony. Do not list devices: explain their specific role in the interaction.

A rhetorical technique does not have one automatic effect: explain what it does in this situation and how the other character responds.

Across all three areas: Do spoken words and stage directions reinforce or contradict each other? How do speech patterns, visual actions and wording develop the interaction? What might the audience understand that a character misses?
04

From notes to a convincing argument

Writing a Coherent Drama Analysis

Make your interpretation the organising principle.

1 · Introduction

Identify the playwright, title, text type, publication/performance context if relevant, and the scene. State the analytical focus and a concise interpretative hypothesis that addresses the task.

2 · Line of Analysis

Organise paragraphs around a meaningful development, relationship or interpretative aspect. You may follow the interaction's progression, but avoid scene-by-scene retelling or isolated lists of techniques.

3 · Analytical paragraphs

Point → Evidence → Technique → Function / Interpretation → Link. Explain how the selected detail contributes to the interaction and your overall interpretation. A stage direction can be as important as a quotation.

4 · Concluding insight

Draw together the main interpretation and, where meaningful, the scene's role in the wider drama. Avoid new evidence and generic claims about audience emotions.

From description to interpretation

ObservationWhat your analysis must add
“The character asks several questions.”Does the questioning show genuine curiosity, uncertainty, pressure, control or a change of attitude? How does the other character respond?
“The character turns away.”What does this non-verbal reaction suggest about feelings, distance, resistance or the relationship at this moment?
“The tone becomes ironic.”Which words or cues indicate irony? Does it conceal criticism, create closeness or provoke a response?

Useful analytical phrases

The interaction initially suggests …, yet as the conversation develops …

While X attempts to …, Y responds by …, which reveals …

The shift from … to … marks a significant change in …

The stage direction indicating … contrasts with … and suggests …

Through X's choice of words / tone / repeated …, the playwright conveys …

Taken together, these details reveal … rather than simply …

Use evidence effectively: Integrate short quotations naturally, add accurate line references and analyse relevant stage directions. Stay in the simple present and use formal, precise wording. Keep referring back to the specific task.

Before you hand it in

  • My opening contains an interpretative hypothesis that answers the task.
  • Each paragraph has a clear analytical point and relevant evidence.
  • I explain what techniques contribute to the scene — not just name them.
  • I address all three specified focus areas where relevant.
  • I trace meaningful changes and underlying tensions without forcing a pattern.
  • My paragraphs build a coherent line of analysis.
  • I integrate short quotations and stage directions with references.
  • I avoid unsupported claims, retelling and generic audience effects.
