METHOD SHEET

Analysing Dramatic Interaction

Your analysis route

Step 01 / 04

Understanding the General Situation

Who is involved, what matters to them, and why?

A convincing drama analysis begins with an overall understanding of the situation. First, make sure you understand what happens in the excerpt, how the characters relate to one another, and what their intentions and perspectives are. This provides the foundation for examining how the interaction unfolds and how dramatic techniques create meaning.

Understand before you analyse

Situation & context

Who is speaking to whom? What has led to this exchange, and why does it take place now? What do the characters know, and what does the task ask you to focus on?

Why does this interaction matter in the play?

Consider what prompts the interaction at this point in the drama and what purpose it may serve. A dialogue does not necessarily move the plot forward immediately. It can also delay a decision, deepen a relationship, build expectations or reveal something important about the characters.

↓
Perspectives & relationships

What does each character want, fear, assume or expect? How close or distant are they? Are power, status or dependency relevant?

↓
Tension & subtext

Are there tensions between the characters? Are feelings or intentions left unspoken?

Conflict is not always an argument

A dramatic dialogue does not need an open quarrel. Dramatic tension may arise from conflicting values, unequal power, hesitation or unspoken expectations.

Yet an interaction can also develop through affection, persuasion, growing understanding or a change of perspective.

What is subtext?

Subtext is meaning that remains implicit: a character may say one thing while wanting, fearing or implying something else. Look for avoidance of direct answers, pauses, contradictions, gestures and patterns of response. Support your interpretation with evidence from the text.

Step 02 / 04

Tracing the Progression

How is the exchange structured, and what changes along the way?

Dramatic dialogues may unfold in very different ways. Examining their structure and progression helps you recognise significant changes. The following framework can help you trace how a dramatic interaction progresses from its starting situation towards an outcome.

Opening
How does the exchange begin? How do the characters initially approach one another, and what tone or expectations are established?
↓
Development
As the characters respond to one another, they may agree, disagree, question, persuade, connect or withdraw. Their attitudes, relationship or the atmosphere may change.
↓
Critical Moment
A significant statement, reaction or action may mark a turning point (a change in direction) or a climax (a peak of tension or emotion). Such a moment can also be subtle, for example a new realisation or a shift in understanding.
↓
Outcome
The exchange ends with the relationship, attitudes or situation changed, reaffirmed or left open. Even an unresolved interaction can influence what happens next.
A useful framework, not a fixed formula!

When analysing dramatic dialogue: Use the framework to explore how the playwright shapes the interaction, but do not force a scene into a predetermined structure. Not every dialogue has a distinct turning point or climax; some have several meaningful shifts, while others unfold quietly or end without a resolution.

When writing your own dramatic dialogue: Use the framework as a guide for creating a convincing exchange, with meaningful responses, changes in the relationship, tension or atmosphere, and a purposeful ending.

Identifying Meaningful Shifts

What changes — and why does it matter?

Pay attention to shifts in tone, emotional distance, initiative, control, expectations, confidence or understanding. Identify where a change becomes noticeable, what brings it about and what it reveals about the characters or their relationship.

Connect structure and technique: Explain how speaking turns, stage directions and language make the change noticeable, and why it matters for the characters or relationship.

Step 03 / 04

How Is It Presented?

Interaction, stage directions and use of language

A convincing interpretation of a dramatic dialogue combines the characters' responses, their actions and their use of language. Analyse features only when they contribute to your interpretation.

What if the task names all three areas?

If an exam task asks you to focus on interaction, stage directions and use of language, consider all three and address each with meaningful evidence. They need not receive equal attention or appear in three separate paragraphs. Explain how they work together to create meaning.

A · Interaction & Communication

How do the characters respond to one another?

  • Turn-taking: Who initiates, answers, speaks at length, dominates or withdraws?
  • Responses: Do they acknowledge, ignore, contradict, evade or build on a remark?
  • Questions, commands and interruptions: Who leads, challenges or pressures the other?
  • Silences and pauses: What is withheld, and how does it affect the exchange?
  • Patterns and shifts: Does the interaction become more affectionate, distant, confrontational, cooperative or uncertain?

Always ask: What does this communicative pattern reveal about the relationship and the development of the dialogue?

B · Stage Directions & Performance

What does the audience see and hear beyond the words?

  • Gestures, facial expressions and posture: What intentions and feelings become visible?
  • Movement, positioning and distance: How are relationships and power relations staged?
  • Pauses, entrances, exits and delivery: What changes in the interaction?
  • Props, costumes and setting: When do objects or physical surroundings carry meaning?
  • Words vs. actions: Do stage directions reinforce or contradict what is said?

Important: Distinguish the playwright's actual stage directions from staging choices you merely imagine. Ground analysis in the text.

C · Use of Language

Language choices matter in context

Examine how word choice, sentence structure, tone and stylistic devices influence the interaction and reveal the characters' attitudes. You already know this toolkit from the three guided scene analyses.

Language toolkit · Syntax, tone, word choice & devices

Explore one category at a time. Use it when it helps explain what happens between the characters.

Syntax and sentence structure

  • Short or clipped sentences may convey urgency or frustration, or suggest that a character is Hesitant or unwilling to do something. to To explain or develop an idea in more detail; to expand on something..
  • Longer or complex sentences may allow a character to explain, justify or develop a thought.
  • Unfinished sentences, interruptions and pauses may suggest hesitation, tension or a disrupted exchange.
  • Parallel structures can create rhythm, connect ideas or emphasise a contrast.

Tone

Tone is the attitude or mood conveyed through a character's words and, in drama, through stage directions. Look for evidence and notice any shifts in tone.

Word fields and choice of words

  • Notice recurring groups of related words (semantic fields).
  • Examine positive or negative connotations and the associations they create.
  • Consider whether the language is formal, colloquial, simple, emotionally charged or noticeably different from the character's usual way of speaking.

From speech analysis to drama: Instead of focusing mainly on how a speaker persuades an audience, explain what the dialogue reveals about the characters, their relationship and the developing conflict.

How do the three areas work together?

Notice the connection between a character’s line, the response it provokes and any accompanying stage directions. For instance, a short answer may suggest distance only when its context, tone or reaction supports that reading. Do not automatically assign one fixed effect to a technique.

Exam principle: Select the strongest evidence across the required areas. A coherent analysis explains function and meaning rather than collecting examples under three disconnected headings.

Step 04 / 04

Writing a Coherent Analysis

From close reading to a line of analysis

Your written analysis should be guided by a clear interpretation of the scene. Use relevant dramatic techniques and textual evidence to develop that interpretation — instead of retelling the dialogue or listing devices.

1 · Introduction & interpretative hypothesis

Introduce the extract and your focus

Identify the playwright, title, text type and scene or extract. Briefly establish the situation only as far as necessary and formulate a thesis that responds to the task: what does the interaction reveal, and how does it develop?

Avoid generic openings about drama, unsubstantiated statements about the audience or a plot summary that delays the analysis.

2 · Build a line of analysis

Organise paragraphs around meaningful interpretative aspects or developments. You may follow the progression of the interaction, but chronological order alone is not a line of analysis. Each paragraph must advance your overall interpretation.

3 · From evidence to explanation

Point → Evidence → Technique → Function → Link

Point

Make a specific analytical claim connected to your thesis.

Evidence

Support it with a brief quotation, linked speaking turns or a meaningful stage direction. Add line references.

Technique & function

Explain how the detail works, what it suggests and what it contributes to the interaction.

Link

Relate your finding to the development of the relationship, the overall interpretation or the wider drama.

Remember: These are thinking steps, not five sentences you must reproduce mechanically. Combine them naturally into clear, connected paragraphs.

Useful analytical language

Phrases for a line of analysis

Develop the interpretation

While the interaction initially appears to …, it gradually reveals …

What begins as … develops into …

Analyse responses

By responding to … with …, the character …

Instead of addressing …, the character …, suggesting …

Link action and words

The stage directions indicating … contrast with … and imply …

This shift in tone becomes apparent when …

Explain function

Through the use of …, the playwright emphasises …

In the context of their relationship, this suggests …

Before handing in

Final check

  • Does my introduction contain a focused interpretative hypothesis?
  • Do my paragraphs build a coherent line of analysis?
  • Have I addressed interaction, stage directions and use of language when requested?
  • Does every major observation have precise textual evidence and an explanation?
  • Do I trace relevant changes, relationships and possible underlying tensions?
  • Have I avoided retelling, device lists and unsupported claims about effects?
  • Are my quotations brief, smoothly integrated and referenced correctly?
