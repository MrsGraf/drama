Step 01 / 04

What is said?

Understanding the conflict before analysing its presentation

Before examining individual techniques, find out what is at stake. A conflict becomes meaningful when we understand what the characters want from each other; and why they struggle to make themselves understood.

Read & explore

Read Walter and Beneatha's exchange, beginning with the stage directions introducing Beneatha. Work through the following questions in order and make notes on your findings.

Identify the different perspectives

What does Walter expect from Beneatha? How does Beneatha defend her ambitions and her view of the insurance money?

Look beneath the disagreement

What are they explicitly arguing about? Distinguish the Surface meaning vs. subtext: The immediate topic is what the characters are explicitly talking about. Subtext refers to what they may mean, feel or want to communicate without saying it directly. of the argument from any deeper expectations or pressures.

Step 02 / 04

How is it said?

Looking closely at interaction, language and stage directions

A dramatic dialogue works through responses. To analyse it, look at how a statement is answered, ignored, challenged or repeated; and what happens as a result.

Close Reading A · Who gets heard?

Focus on Turn-taking describes how speaking turns are organised in a conversation: who speaks, for how long, whether someone interrupts, and how the speakers respond to one another.
Pattern of turn-taking refers to a noticeable or recurring way in which the speaking turns are distributed and linked.

Examine how Walter and Beneatha respond to one another. Notice when they anticipate, challenge or dismiss each other's remarks.

Close Reading B · Words and actions

Examining the use of language

Language toolkit · Syntax, tone, word choice & devices

Explore one category at a time. Use it when it helps explain what happens between the characters.

Syntax and sentence structure

  • Short or clipped sentences may convey urgency or frustration, or suggest that a character is Hesitant or unwilling to do something. to To explain or develop an idea in more detail; to expand on something..
  • Longer or complex sentences may allow a character to explain, justify or develop a thought.
  • Unfinished sentences, interruptions and pauses may suggest hesitation, tension or a disrupted exchange.
  • Parallel structures can create rhythm, connect ideas or emphasise a contrast.

Tone

Tone is the attitude or mood conveyed through a character's words and, in drama, through stage directions. Look for evidence and notice any shifts in tone.

Word fields and choice of words

  • Notice recurring groups of related words (semantic fields).
  • Examine positive or negative connotations and the associations they create.
  • Consider whether the language is formal, colloquial, simple, emotionally charged or noticeably different from the character's usual way of speaking.

Step 03 / 04

Staging the Conflict

Understanding dialogue as a stage performance

You have examined individual exchanges and how words work with actions. Now consider the scene as a whole, focusing on what the audience sees as well as what it hears.

From page to stage

A play is written to be performed, not merely read. Stage directions provide clues about the characters' appearance, movements and behaviour. These visual elements can shape the audience's understanding of the conflict, even when the characters are not speaking.

1 · Explore the visual staging Staging refers to how a scene is presented on stage. A playwright's stage directions provide evidence about appearance, gestures, movement, entrances and exits. Individual productions may interpret these directions differently.

Examine Hansberry's stage directions throughout the extract. How are the characters presented on stage? Consider their:

appearance and attitudepositioning and movementactions and reactionsentrances and exits

2 · Explain the dramatic function

Choose two or three significant observations. How does Hansberry use the characters' appearance, actions and movements, including the behaviour of those who are not speaking, to shape the audience's understanding of the conflict between Walter and Beneatha? Support your interpretation with evidence from the stage directions.

Need a small nudge?

Consider Ruth's presence and interventions, how Beneatha enters the scene, or Walter's departure. Look at what the audience can see in addition to the spoken words.

Think critically

Focus on what Hansberry's stage directions actually support. Do not invent precise positions or movements that the text does not specify.
